\title{causalgeo: concurrent geo holdouts for bundle lift and calibrated channel responses}

\author[aff1]{Aditya Puttaparthi Tirumala\corref{cor1}}
\ead{puttaparthy.aditya@gmail.com}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}
\address[aff1]{Independent Researcher}

\begin{abstract}
Marketing teams pause different bundles of channels in different groups of markets at the same time to get enough power. Comparing a group with business-as-usual markets gives the incremental sales, or any other business KPI, of that bundle. It does not show how much of that effect was driven by each channel. A marketing-mix model can divide sales across channels, but that division is unreliable when channels show multicollinearity. \texttt{causalgeo} is a Python package for this test. It reports one lift for each bundle and checks whether a channel left running changed in the paused markets. It estimates how many sales each impression adds for each channel, then shifts those numbers so they add up close to the measured bundle lift. If two channels are too highly correlated to estimate apart, it reports no number for either. We tested it on Google's public Meridian impression paths, with sales we built so the true responses were known. Assuming each impression adds the same sales with no diminishing returns, the recovered responses were within 0.21 percent of the true values. On a simulated 30-week panel, 95 percent intervals covered the true bundle lift in 91.8 percent of 200 runs. When markets respond differently, intervals covered the truth in 34.4 percent of runs, a sales driver the test does not pause made one channel's response about 50 percent too high, and leaving out carryover or saturation gave large channel errors. We did not analyze a real campaign or compare any other tool.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and significance}
\label{sec:motivation}

A geo test estimates incrementality by comparing markets that receive a media change with similar markets left on business as usual \cite{vaver2011,vaver2012}. Comparing markets avoids the bias that comes from lining up regions that got an ad against regions that did not \cite{lewis2011,gordon2019}, and it asks whether the channel created incremental sales \cite{blake2015}.

In practice only some markets can be paused. Consider a United States campaign across 210 designated market areas (DMAs) where the business allows a pause in only 30. Advertising lift is hard to calculate because it is small next to the variance in existing sales \cite{lewisrao2015}, so a well-powered test is needed. Teams therefore bundle channels and run several combinations at once. Each combination is assigned to its own cell: a group of markets that pause that combination, which is the holdout for that combination, compared with markets left on business as usual. Each cell gives the incremental sales of its bundle. This paper uses sales as the outcome, but the design works for any business KPI measured by market and week, such as sign-ups or store visits. It does not show how much of the effect each channel drove.

Existing packages GeoLift and Meridian GeoX can design and estimate more than one cell \cite{geolift,geox2026}. A mix model estimates channel response from impression history, and when channels move together that allocation is arbitrary \cite{chanperry2017,jin2017}. Robyn and PyMC-Marketing can calibrate a mix model towards a lift test \cite{runge2024,pymcmarketing2024}. Table~\ref{tab:packages} says what each package does. We did not run them on the same data, so the table is not a score. Calibration can adjust the channel estimates closer to the true channel effects \cite{zhang2023}.

\texttt{causalgeo} estimates the bundle lift from the holdout, then calibrates a model in which each extra impression adds the same number of sales. The channel responses therefore come from both the test and the model, not from a channel-level holdout. When channels move together and are highly correlated, the package reports only the bundle lift and gives no number for those channels. It leaves spend during the test out of the model, because the pause is what changes that spend \cite{angrist2009}.

\section{Software description}
\label{sec:software}

\subsection{Software architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\begin{sloppypar}
\texttt{design\_\allowbreak concurrent\_\allowbreak test} randomizes which markets pause. \texttt{disen\-tangle} takes that assignment, the outcomes, and the impressions. It estimates each cell's bundle lift and allocates it across channels, and gives no number for a channel the test cannot estimate on its own. \texttt{design.py} assigns the markets, \texttt{identified.py} estimates the cell lift, \texttt{exposure.py} checks channels left on, and \texttt{calibrated.py} fits the split.
\end{sloppypar}

The bundle estimate subtracts each market's own average and each week's average from sales. When a market is missing some weeks, the code alternates these two subtractions until both averages settle, and a week with a missing outcome is dropped. Standard errors are clustered by market \cite{wooldridge2010,bertrand2004,cameron2015}. The example has 40 markets with 8 paused markets in each cell. On a simulated 30-week panel of that size, we simulated the assignment 200 times, and the stated 95 percent intervals covered the true cell lift 91.8 percent of the time, slightly below 95 percent. The Monte Carlo interval around that share is 89.7 to 94.0 percent (\texttt{paper/cell\_coverage.py}). For a single cell that is already assigned, \texttt{analyze} can also run time-based regression \cite{kerman2017}, synthetic control \cite{abadie2010,xu2017}, or synthetic difference-in-differences \cite{arkhangelsky2021}. Synthetic control matches the paused markets before the test with a weighted average of the others. Its interval uses only the pre-test miss and leaves out shocks during the test. In simulation it covered the truth about half the time, so the package warns that it is not a confidence interval.

\subsection{Software functionalities}
\label{sec:function}

\paragraph{Design}
The package randomizes the holdout. The user supplies the markets that may be used, which of them are similar, the channel list, and which channels pause together in each cell. Within each group of similar markets, it randomly keeps a fraction {\ttfamily control\_\allowbreak share} (default 0.5) on business as usual and randomly assigns the rest to the cells, as evenly as the group allows. The markets assigned to a cell are the holdout for that combination. The markets kept on are the business-as-usual regions. It does not choose eligible markets, similar groups, or combinations. If the user already has the assignment, {\ttfamily design\_\allowbreak from\_\allowbreak assignment} accepts it with the same cell labels. The package then labels each channel: not tested if no cell pauses it, tied if it is always paused with another channel, separate if the cells can estimate it on its own, and not separable otherwise. A channel that is not tested gets no estimate.

\paragraph{Bundle lift}
$D_{it}^{(k)}=1$ when market $i$ is in cell $k$ and week $t$ is in the test window. The cell estimate is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cell}
Y_{it} = \sum_{k} \tau_{k}\, D_{it}^{(k)} + \alpha_{i} + \gamma_{t} + \varepsilon_{it}.
\end{equation}
The outcome $Y_{it}$ is sales for market $i$ in week $t$. The formulas say sales, but any business KPI measured at that level works, and the responses below are then in that KPI's units. $\tau_{k}$ is the incremental outcome of cell $k$ after removing each market's baseline and each week's common movement. It is a channel estimate only when the cell paused a single channel. The test does not show how much of it each channel drove.

\paragraph{Channels left on}
A channel left on in cell $k$ can still deliver differently in that cell's markets. The impression gap is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:shift}
S_{kc} = \big(\bar X_{c,k,\mathrm{post}} - \bar X_{c,k,\mathrm{pre}}\big) - \big(\bar X_{c,\mathrm{control},\mathrm{post}} - \bar X_{c,\mathrm{control},\mathrm{pre}}\big).
\end{equation}
The pre-test weight $b_{c}$ is the joint pre-test response of channel $c$, from a regression of pre-test sales on all channel impressions with market and week effects. It is not a one-channel estimate. The published cell lift remains $\tau_{k}$. If that gap, valued at $b_{c}$, is larger than \texttt{material} (default 0.10) times the larger of $|\tau_{k}|$ and the standard deviation of pre-test sales across all markets and weeks, the channel allocation uses a refit that accounts for the impressions that were meant to stay on. The user can publish the refit as the cell lift instead. The example table shows that refit.

\paragraph{Channel allocation}
$W_{kc}$ is the impressions of channel $c$ held out of cell $k$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:withheld}
W_{kc} = \max(-S_{kc},\, 0)
\end{equation}
for the channels that the cell paused. Every other entry in that row is zero. The mix model is asked to make $\sum_{c} \beta_{c} W_{kc}$ close to $-\tau_{k}$, where $\beta_{c}$ is the sales per impression, assumed the same at every level of delivery. Each $\beta_{c}$ is set to zero when the fit would otherwise say that more impressions produce less of the outcome. The bundle lift $\tau_{k}$ is not set to zero. Calibrating towards the lift test shifts these numbers so they add up close to the measured lift. It is not a hard constraint. The impression history uses every pre-period week, but during the test it uses only the business-as-usual markets, so paused market-weeks are left out. The bundle table shows whatever gap remains.

The reported uncertainty treats each cell's lift as if the cells did not share markets.

When two channels' impressions are almost perfectly correlated (absolute cosine similarity above $1-\epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ is \texttt{copy\_\allowbreak tolerance}, default $10^{-6}$), their effects cannot be told apart. The package keeps their combined impressions in the model as a control and reports no number for either channel. A response is marked unstable if it moves when the calibration towards the test is weakened, or when the market with the largest sales during the test is left out. The tables below switch the check off to show point estimates. A missing impression drops its market-week, and the fit warns. Daily impact is the delivered impressions times the fitted response. The package does not fit a curve in which the next impression is worth less, because a single on-or-off holdout cannot trace that curve \cite{hanssens2001,jin2017}.

\paragraph{One cell}
One cell measures a single number, the bundle lift $\tau_{k}$. The held-out impressions $W_{kc}$ show how many impressions of each channel were removed, not what each one was worth. Call that unknown weight $p_{c}$, in sales per impression. The holdout gives one equation,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:one-bundle}
\sum_{c} p_{c} W_{kc} = -\tau_{k}.
\end{equation}
With several channels in one cell there are more unknowns than equations, so one cell cannot identify $p_{c}$. The calibrated responses $\beta_{c}$ play the same role, but they come from several cells and the impression history, as the multi-cell fit below explains.

A user who already has $p_{c}$, from an older single-channel test or a mix model they trust, can allocate the measured lift as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:allocation}
a_{kc} = \tau_{k}\,\frac{p_{c} W_{kc}}{\sum_{j} p_{j} W_{kj}}.
\end{equation}
The channel pieces add up to the cell lift. Setting every $p_{c}$ equal to 1 allocates the bundle by held-out impressions, and any other weight is prior knowledge the user encodes. We tested this on the public Meridian impression paths with all five channels paused together and sales built from known weights. An allocation by impressions gives Channel2 7.8 percent of the bundle against a true share of 2.4 percent, and Channel1 12.7 percent against 19.2 percent. Putting the known weights into \eqref{eq:allocation} closes both gaps. This uses the weights that built the sales, so the holdout does not discover them. The script is \texttt{examples/public\_allocation\_benchmark.py}, and the test suite runs it.

The multi-cell fit is different. When different cells pause different channels, the impressions vary enough to estimate each channel. Equation \eqref{eq:allocation} only shows how the user divided one lift, and it is not an estimate of each channel's incrementality.

The output lists the channel responses, the measured and implied bundle lifts, the check for channels left on, and daily impact. A market bootstrap reruns the whole fit on markets redrawn within each cell.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:example}

No public geo holdout comes with a known channel-level incrementality. The example therefore uses Google's published Meridian geo file \path{geo_all_channels.csv}, which has 40 markets and 156 weeks from 25 January 2021 through 15 January 2024 \cite{meridiandata}. The impressions come from that file and the sales are constructed so that the channel responses are known. The example checks the software and says nothing about whether a campaign worked. We call the outcome sales, but it could be any business KPI. Meridian's conversions column only groups similar markets. It is not the outcome, because that series already contains Meridian's own media response.

We use three of the five channels, with true responses set at $2\times 10^{-4}$, $5\times 10^{-4}$, and $1\times 10^{-4}$ sales per impression. Markets are grouped into quartiles of average conversions. With \texttt{control\_share = 0.4} and seed 1, the package assigns the 40 markets to the four groups in Table~\ref{tab:strategy}. The test window is the last 16 weeks, starting 2 October 2023. In a paused market-week the paused channel's impressions are set to zero, and sales are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sales}
Y_{it} = \alpha_{i} + \gamma_{t} + \sum_{c} \beta_{c} X_{itc} + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
with $\alpha_{i}\sim N(0,50^{2})$, $\gamma_{t}\sim N(0,20^{2})$, and $\varepsilon_{it}\sim N(0,30^{2})$, drawn with seed 7. The script is \texttt{paper/example.py}.

\section{Impact}
\label{sec:impact}

The users are marketing analytics teams that run geo tests. In the campaign of Section~\ref{sec:motivation}, the business has 210 designated market areas and may pause only 30. The package lets the team pause several combinations in that pool. Each cell is the holdout for one combination and returns the incremental sales of that bundle. The team supplies the markets, the groups of similar markets, and the combinations. The package randomizes which markets pause and allocates each bundle across its channels. GeoLift and GeoX judge whether a test can detect the lift \cite{geolift,geox2026}. The team gains several bundle holdouts, the gap to the model, and no channel number where the test cannot support one.

The current release is 0.2.4. Adoption is early and this paper includes no user study. A real holdout needs only market outcomes and channel impressions as inputs.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

\texttt{causalgeo} estimates holdouts of several channel bundles at once and allocates a bundle across channels only when their impressions differ enough to estimate each one. Otherwise it reports no channel numbers, or the user supplies the split.

Where the channel numbers go wrong, a response that differs by market cuts coverage to 34.4 percent, and a sales driver the test does not pause keeps that response about 50 percent high.

A channel response is not a holdout estimate, and allocating a bundle lift needs prior knowledge the user encodes. The model assumes each impression adds the same sales, with no diminishing returns. The package does not fit a curve in which the next impression is worth less \cite{jin2017}, and it does not allow channels to interact \cite{naik2003}. Power analysis is not in this release. The package makes no claim that eight markets are enough to power a cell \cite{lewisrao2015}. The synthetic-control interval is not a confidence interval, and the cell intervals treat cells as if they did not share markets. Spend during the test is left out because the pause changes it \cite{angrist2009,lewis2011}. We did not analyze a real campaign. We did not run GeoLift, GeoX, Robyn, or PyMC-Marketing on the same data.
